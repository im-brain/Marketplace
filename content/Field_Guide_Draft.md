## Dry rocks lie

Every Great Lakes beach is a pile of glacial leftovers: billion-year-old lava, coral reefs from a Devonian sea, chunks of Canada, and a few things people made by accident. Dry, most of it looks like the same tan gravel. Wet, it tells the truth. Water fills the tiny scratches on a stone's skin so light goes in instead of bouncing off, and the real color, banding, and pattern show up.

This guide covers the stones you are actually likely to pick up, how to see them, and where you are allowed to keep them. `[Shirt: Great Lakes Beach Stone Chart]`

## How to look

- **Wet everything.** Walk the wave line, or carry a spray bottle. A stone that looks dull dry and glows wet is worth a second look. Minnesota DNR notes agates look richest in color when wet. ([MN DNR, Agate Hounds](https://files.dnr.state.mn.us/mcvmagazine/young_naturalists/young-naturalists-article/agate_hounds/agate_hounds.pdf))
- **Put the sun in front of you.** Translucent stones (agate, beach glass, some quartz) light up when the sun shines through them toward you. Minnesota DNR says sunny days are best for seeing translucence. ([MN DNR, Lake Superior Agate](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/education/geology/digging/agate.html)) The "low sun, early or late" part is standard hunter advice. [VERIFY: no official source found for low-angle sun specifically]
- **Go after a storm.** Big waves turn over the gravel and leave fresh stones on top. Michigan EGLE gives the same tip for agates. ([EGLE, agate tips, 2024](https://www.michigan.gov/egle/newsroom/mi-environment/2024/07/01/egle-staffer-offers-tips-for-finding-lake-superior-agates))
- **Spring ice-out.** Shore ice and spring storms rework the beach, so the first warm weeks after the ice leaves can be good. Michigan Tech geologists say winter ice pulls stones from deeper water onto the shore. ([Michigan Tech Geology, outreach blog](https://blogs.mtu.edu/geo/category/outreach/page/3/))
- **Look for the right gravel.** Pick beaches with lots of exposed pebbles, not pure sand. ([EGLE, agate tips, 2024](https://www.michigan.gov/egle/newsroom/mi-environment/2024/07/01/egle-staffer-offers-tips-for-finding-lake-superior-agates))
- **For the glowing stones, go at night** with a 365 nm UV flashlight, UV-blocking glasses, and a normal flashlight for the walk back. ([EGLE, glowing stone tips, 2023](https://www.michigan.gov/egle/newsroom/mi-environment/2023/06/05/egle-rockhound-offers-tips-to-find-elusive-glowing-yooperlites))

## The stones

### 1. Lake Superior agate

**What it is:** Banded chalcedony (very fine-grained quartz) that filled gas bubbles in lava about 1.1 billion years ago, colored red, orange, and yellow by iron. It is Minnesota's state gemstone (1969).

**Wet vs dry:** Dry, it is a dull, waxy, often pitted red-brown pebble that hides in plain sight. Wet, it turns glossy and slightly see-through at the edges, and you may catch thin parallel lines (the bands) on a broken face. Hold it up to the sun: a little light should pass through.

**Where:** Lake Superior beaches in Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Michigan's Upper Peninsula (Grand Marais, Whitefish Point, and Eagle River are often named), plus glacial gravel pits well south of the lake.

**Lookalike:** Red jasper and chert. Both are opaque (no light passes through), and they lack bands. Plain vein quartz passes light but looks glassy, not waxy. Agates also often show a pitted "orange peel" skin and curved, shell-like chips.

**Field note:** The only red rock on the beach that will let a little daylight through. Everyone else is bluffing.

### 2. Petoskey stone

**What it is:** A fossil colony of the coral *Hexagonaria percarinata* from a Devonian tropical sea, now Michigan's state stone (Act 89 of 1965).

**Wet vs dry:** This is the textbook "dry rocks lie" stone. Dry, it looks like a plain gray or tan limestone pebble, maybe with faint mottling. Wet, a pattern of tightly packed six-sided cells jumps out, each with a dark "eye" in the center and thin lines radiating out like a starburst.

**Where:** Lake Michigan beaches of the northern Lower Peninsula, especially Little Traverse Bay (Petoskey), and the Traverse City to Charlevoix shoreline, plus inland gravel. Glaciers pulled them from Devonian bedrock (Traverse Group) and spread them around. Lake Huron shores near Alpena are also mentioned. [VERIFY: Lake Huron occurrence is weakly sourced]

**Lookalike:** The Charlevoix stone (next entry). Petoskey cells are bigger, each with a dark center dot and radiating lines.

**Field note:** Michigan's state stone has to be licked before it will introduce itself.

### 3. Charlevoix stone (and how to tell it from a Petoskey)

**What it is:** A fossil "honeycomb" coral of the genus *Favosites*, from the same Middle Devonian rocks as the Petoskey stone, but a different coral entirely.

**Wet vs dry:** Dry, another gray pebble. Wet, a fine honeycomb of small, tightly packed cells appears, usually with no center dot and no radiating lines. On broken pieces you may see stacked "ladder rungs" (tabulae) running along each tube.

**Where:** Same northern Lake Michigan beaches as Petoskey stones; commonly associated with the Charlevoix area.

**Telling them apart (the quick test):**

| | Petoskey | Charlevoix |
|---|---|---|
| Genus | *Hexagonaria* | *Favosites* |
| Cell size | Larger | Smaller, finer |
| Center of each cell | Dark eye | Usually empty or plain |
| Radiating lines | Yes, starburst | No |

**Lookalike:** Each other. Also fossil-free mottled limestone, which never shows a regular cell pattern when wet.

**Field note:** Same reef, same sea, smaller apartments.

Sources:
- [University of Michigan Museum of Paleontology, MI Backyard Fossils: Corals](https://umorf.ummp.lsa.umich.edu/wp/mi-backyard-fossils-corals/)
- [Visit Charlevoix, Petoskey and Charlevoix stones](https://www.visitcharlevoix.com/petoskey_and_charlevoix_stones) (tourism source, used only for the local name)

### 4. Leland Blue (slag, not a natural stone)

**What it is:** Leland Blue is not a rock. It is glassy waste (slag) from an iron smelting furnace that the Leland Lake Superior Iron Company ran in Leland, Michigan, starting in 1870, and the slag was dumped along the Lake Michigan shore.

**Wet vs dry:** Dry, it can look dull blue-gray. Wet, it shows strong blues, purples, grays, and greens often with swirls. Look for gas bubbles of mixed sizes, glassy breaks, rusty spots, and pale specks: all signs of something that was once molten in a furnace.

**Where:** Lake Michigan beaches around Leland in Leelanau County.

**Lookalike:** Blue beach glass, which has even color, a frosted skin, and an even thickness. Leland Blue is lumpy, often bubbly, and streaky. Natural blue stones are rare on these beaches, so a bright blue "rock" is almost always slag or glass.

**Field note:** Michigan's most beloved rock is a 150-year-old industrial accident. We love it anyway.

*Fact flag for customers: say "slag" plainly. Some sellers call it a gemstone; it is not.*

Sources:
- [Fishtown Preservation, Leland Blue and the Iron Works history (2024)](https://fishtownmi.org/2024/04/leland-blue-and-the-iron-works-history)
- [9&10 News, Leland Blue: manmade, not quite on purpose (2023)](https://www.9and10news.com/2023/07/14/leland-blue-manmade-not-quite-on-purpose-and-in-limited-supply/)
- [Washington University, Glassy and vesicular = slag](https://sites.wustl.edu/meteoritesite/items/slag) (general slag ID)

### 5. UV-glowing sodalite-bearing syenite

**What it is:** A gray, granite-like igneous rock called syenite that carries the mineral sodalite, which glows bright orange-yellow under longwave ultraviolet light. Its presence on Lake Superior beaches was reported in 2017 to 2018, and testing traced the glow to sodalite.

**Wet vs dry:** This one breaks the brand rule. In daylight, wet or dry, it is an ordinary speckled gray stone. The truth only comes out in the dark: under a 365 nm UV light, the sodalite grains glow in spots and streaks. Wet stones work fine; turning stones and raking the gravel matters more, since a grain may only glow from one side.

**Where:** Michigan's Lake Superior shore, especially between Whitefish Point and Grand Marais, and the Keweenaw Peninsula. Michigan EGLE says most are pinky-nail to thumb size. The stones are thought to be glacial imports from Canada.

**Lookalike:** Other things on the beach glow too: some calcite, fishing line, plastic bits, and lint. Real sodalite glow sits inside a gray, grainy rock and appears as patchy orange-yellow spots, not a smooth even glow.

**Field note:** A plain gray rock with a secret, revealed only to people who walk the beach at midnight with a flashlight.

*Collecting flag: Pictured Rocks National Lakeshore begins just west of Grand Marais (the Grand Sable Dunes and Au Sable Light are inside it), and taking stones there is illegal. Know the boundary before you rake.* [VERIFY: confirm the exact east boundary on an NPS map]

Sources:
- [Michigan EGLE, tips to find glowing sodalite syenite (2023)](https://www.michigan.gov/egle/newsroom/mi-environment/2023/06/05/egle-rockhound-offers-tips-to-find-elusive-glowing-yooperlites)
- [Mental Floss, Michigan rock hunter discovers glowing stones (2018)](https://mentalfloss.com/article/556551/michigan-rock-hunter-discovers-glowing-yooperlites-lake-superior-beach) (news account of the 2017 find; reports differ on whether Michigan Tech or Michigan State arranged the testing)

### 6. Unakite

**What it is:** An altered granite made of pink feldspar, pistachio-green epidote, and clear to gray quartz.

**Wet vs dry:** Dry, it reads as a muddy pink-and-olive pebble. Wet, the salmon pink and pistachio green separate into crisp, high-contrast patches.

**Where:** Glacial pebbles on Lake Superior beaches, with reports from Lakes Huron and Michigan. [VERIFY: Lake Huron and Lake Michigan occurrence comes from hobby sources]

**Lookalike:** Ordinary pink granite with dark green-black specks (hornblende or chlorite). True unakite green is a yellowish pistachio, not near-black.

**Field note:** Pink and green together, which on most beaches would be a fashion choice. Here it is geology.

Sources:
- [Rock and Gem, Exploring Unakite](https://www.rockngem.com/exploring-unakite/)
- [VERIFY: no state survey page found for unakite on Great Lakes beaches]

### 7. Thomsonite

**What it is:** A zeolite mineral that grew as radiating fibers inside gas pockets of North Shore lava, forming round "eyes" of pink, white, red, and green.

**Wet vs dry:** Dry, a thomsonite pebble may look like a basalt stone with chalky pale dots. Wet, the dots turn into concentric ringed eyes with a silky sheen.

**Where:** Minnesota's North Shore near Grand Marais (Good Harbor Bay and the Thomsonite Beach area) in Cook County. Wisconsin's survey also reports thomsonite near the Montreal River in Iron County. Much of the famous Thomsonite Beach shoreline is private. [VERIFY: access status]

**Lookalike:** Agate "eyes" (translucent, waxy) and other white zeolites. Thomsonite is opaque with a silky, fibrous look and color in rings.

**Field note:** A stone that stares back. Politely.

Sources:
- [Wisconsin Geological and Natural History Survey, Thomsonite-Ca](https://home.wgnhs.wisc.edu/minerals/thomsonite-ca/)
- [Rock and Gem, Minnesota Thomsonite](https://www.rockngem.com/radiant-minnesota-thomsonite/)

### 8. Chlorastrolite (Isle Royale greenstone)

**What it is:** A green variety of the mineral pumpellyite that formed in gas pockets of Keweenawan lava, and Michigan's state gem (1972).

**Wet vs dry:** Dry, beach pebbles are small, dull green specks in basalt. Wet, the best ones show a "turtleback" mosaic of dark and light green with a silky shimmer that slides across the surface as you tilt it.

**Where:** Isle Royale, where collecting is illegal (see rules below), and the Keweenaw Peninsula, mostly in old copper mine rock piles and some beaches. Most pebbles are a quarter inch or smaller.

**Lookalike:** Plain green amygdule fillings (epidote, prehnite, or green pumpellyite without the pattern). No turtleback pattern and no moving shimmer means no chlorastrolite.

**Field note:** Michigan's state gem is usually smaller than a pencil eraser and legally off limits in its best spot. It plays hard to get.

**Collecting is illegal in Isle Royale National Park**, on land and in the Lake Superior waters of the park.

Sources:
- [NPS Isle Royale, Laws and Policies](https://www.nps.gov/isro/learn/management/lawsandpolicies.htm)
- [NPS Isle Royale, Geologic Resource Evaluation Report (2008, PDF)](https://www.nps.gov/isro/learn/management/upload/ISRO_Web_Accessible_Geologic_Resource_Evaluation_2008.pdf)

### 9. Puddingstone (jasper conglomerate)

**What it is:** A rock made of rounded red jasper pebbles (plus some black and other chert) cemented in white to pale quartzite, from the Lorrain Formation of Ontario's Bruce Mines area, carried south by glaciers.

**Wet vs dry:** Dry, the white matrix and the pebbles blur into a dusty pink. Wet, the jasper turns cherry red against bright white, the "cherries in suet pudding" look that gave it the name.

**Where:** Drummond Island and the eastern Upper Peninsula, the Lake Huron shore, and as glacial erratics scattered across Michigan's Lower Peninsula.

**Lookalike:** Other conglomerates and gravelly concrete. True puddingstone has hard, glassy quartzite holding mostly red jasper clasts. Concrete has gray cement, mixed random gravel, and often sand voids.

**Field note:** A fruitcake that the glaciers delivered personally.

Sources:
- [University of Waterloo Earth Sciences Museum, Lorrain Formation jasper conglomerate](https://uwaterloo.ca/earth-sciences-museum/peter-russell-rock-garden/1-lorrain-formation-jasper-conglomerate)

### 10. Basalt with amygdules

**What it is:** Dark lava rock whose gas bubbles were later filled by minerals such as quartz, calcite, epidote, chlorite, prehnite, or zeolites, leaving filled spots called amygdules.

**Wet vs dry:** Dry, a dark gray-brown cobble with faint light freckles. Wet, the freckles pop: white, pink, green, and sometimes agate-orange spots against near-black. On beach pebbles the fillings often stand up as knobs because the softer lava around them wears away first.

**Where:** Lake Superior beaches throughout, especially the Keweenaw Peninsula and Minnesota's North Shore. This is the rock agates, thomsonite, and chlorastrolite grow in.

**Lookalike:** Vesicular basalt (empty bubbles, no fillings) and spotted granite. Basalt is fine-grained and dark; granite shows interlocking light crystals all over.

**Field note:** Not the prize, but the prize's childhood home. Check every freckle.

Sources:
- [USGS, Amygdaloidal basalt on Great Sand Bay beach, Keweenaw](https://usgs.gov/media/images/amygdaloidal-basalt-rock-great-sand-bay-beach-keweenaw-peninsula-michigan)

- [Carleton College geology collection, North Shore amygdaloidal basalt](https://bereket-haileab.geology.sites.carleton.edu/items/show/209)

### 11. Jasper

**What it is:** Opaque, fine-grained quartz colored by iron, usually brick red, sometimes yellow or brown.

**Wet vs dry:** Dry, a flat matte red. Wet, a deep, even red with a smooth, almost porcelain look. Hold it to the sun: no light comes through.

**Where:** Common on Lake Superior and Lake Huron beaches as glacial pebbles, including pieces eroded from puddingstone and banded iron formation. [VERIFY: no single survey source gives jasper's beach distribution]

**Lookalike:** Lake Superior agate (translucent, waxy, banded) and red granite (visible crystals). Red sandstone is grainy and gritty.

**Field note:** The most common red rock that people hope is an agate. Love it for what it is.

Sources:

### 12. Epidote

**What it is:** A pistachio to olive green mineral that forms when heat and fluids alter igneous rock; on Lake Superior it fills amygdules and veins in basalt and gives unakite its green.

**Wet vs dry:** Dry, a dusty olive. Wet, a clear, bright yellow-green that is hard to mistake once you have seen it.

**Where:** In basalt and altered granite pebbles around Lake Superior; also in unakite wherever unakite turns up.

**Lookalike:** Chlorite, a darker, softer, often scaly or greasy green mineral that a knife scratches easily. Epidote is harder (about 6 to 7 on the Mohs scale) and has that yellow cast. Green beach glass is translucent and even in color.

**Field note:** The green in half the "is this jade?" stones on Lake Superior. It is not jade. It is still nice.

Sources:
- [Geology of the Western Lake Superior District (UW Digital Collections)](https://search.library.wisc.edu/digital/AOO7SGPDTGAFD48K/text/AB37PJKC5L3GLL8L) (historic survey report describing epidote amygdules)

### 13. Banded iron formation (jaspilite)

**What it is:** Nearly 2-billion-year-old sedimentary rock of alternating red jasper and silvery-black hematite layers from Michigan's iron ranges, such as the Negaunee Iron Formation at Jasper Knob in Ishpeming.

**Wet vs dry:** Dry, the hematite bands look dull gray-black and the jasper looks brick red. Wet, the jasper goes blood red and the hematite flashes metallic silver, so the stripes look painted.

**Where:** The Marquette Iron Range near Ishpeming and Negaunee, and as pebbles on nearby Lake Superior beaches and in glacial gravel. [VERIFY: beach occurrence near Marquette]

**Lookalike:** Banded agate. Agate bands curve in rings around a center and pass light; iron formation bands run flat and parallel (or tightly folded), are opaque, and the stone feels heavy for its size. Hematite leaves a red-brown streak on unglazed tile.

**Field note:** Rust that is roughly 800 million years older than the agates.

Sources:
- [Grand Valley State University, Jasper's Knob tour stop](https://faculty.gvsu.edu/PETERSVI/MarquetteArea/tour/MQ7.htm)
- [Michigan State University EES, Banded Iron Formation](https://ees.natsci.msu.edu/about/bif.aspx)
- [Northwestern University, Negaunee Iron Formation field trip](https://www.earth.northwestern.edu/public/craig/teach/C02-0/jasper.html)
- [VERIFY: age. Published dates for the Negaunee range from about 1.87 to 2.11 billion years; the field note assumes about 1.87 to 1.9 billion against about 1.1 billion for the agates]

### 14. Copper (float copper)

**What it is:** Native (pure, natural) copper torn from Keweenaw bedrock by glaciers and dropped in glacial deposits as the ice melted; locals call these glacially moved pieces "float copper."

**Wet vs dry:** Honest exception: water does not help much. Beach copper is usually coated green or brown-black, so it looks like a dark, lumpy pebble wet or dry. What gives it away is weight (very heavy for its size), a lumpy, dented shape instead of a smooth round one, and a bright copper color where it is scratched.

**Where:** Glacial deposits of the western Upper Peninsula, especially around the Keweenaw; float copper has turned up in every state that received glacial drift from the Lake Superior region. Small beach pieces on Keweenaw shores are reported by collectors. [VERIFY: beach occurrence; the museum sources discuss glacial deposits, not beaches]

**Lookalike:** Rusty iron scrap and slag, which are often magnetic. Native copper is not magnetic and shows copper color when scratched.

**Field note:** The one stone on the beach that is secretly a coin.

Sources:
- [A.E. Seaman Mineral Museum (Michigan Tech), Float Copper in Michigan (2017, PDF)](https://museum.mtu.edu/sites/default/files/2020-12/AESMM_Web_Pub_3_Float_Copper_Michigan_2017.pdf)
- [Michigan Tech Keweenaw Geoheritage, Float Copper](https://www.geo.mtu.edu/KeweenawGeoheritage/CalumetGeosites/Float_Copper.html)

### 15. Beach glass (not a stone)

**What it is:** Broken bottle and jar glass rounded by waves and sand until its surface turns frosted.

**Wet vs dry:** The reverse lie. Wet, it glows like a gem and can fool you into thinking you found agate or something rare. Dry, it shows its frosted, cloudy skin. If it looks clear and jewel-like only while wet, and the color is perfectly even, think glass.

**Where:** Every Great Lakes shoreline, best near old towns, harbors, and former dump sites.

**Lookalike:** Agate and chalcedony. Glass has even thickness, an even color, sometimes a curve or a molded seam from the bottle, and sometimes tiny round bubbles. Agate is waxy, irregular, and banded.

**Field note:** Somebody's 1950s soda bottle, polished by the lake for free. Not a rock, still a keeper.

Sources:
- [Corcoran et al., sea glass weathering study (Journal of Sedimentary Research)](https://archives.datapages.com/data/sepm/journals/080/080010/884_gsjsedres800884.htm) (ocean study; freshwater frosting is assumed similar) [VERIFY]
- [Beachcombing Magazine, The Science of Sea Glass](https://www.beachcombingmagazine.com/blogs/news/the-science-of-sea-glass)

## Collecting rules

Rules change. Check the managing agency before you pocket anything. **This section is a summary, not legal advice.**

### National parks and lakeshores: look, photograph, and leave it

Federal rules (36 CFR 2.1) generally prohibit removing rocks, minerals, and fossils from National Park Service land. That covers:

- **Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore (MI):** NPS says removing stones from the lakeshore is illegal under federal law. Petoskey stones stay. ([NPS, Peterson Beach](https://www.nps.gov/places/000/peterson-beach.htm); [NPS Sleeping Bear, Beaches](https://www.nps.gov/slbe/learn/nature/beaches.htm))
- **Pictured Rocks National Lakeshore (MI):** Collecting is prohibited. Four visitors who carved the cliffs in 2025 and gathered over 100 pounds of rock were each fined $500 and banned for a year, and one paid another $250 for taking the rocks (announced March 2026). ([NPS Pictured Rocks, Superintendent's Compendium](https://www.nps.gov/piro/learn/management/suptcompendium.htm); [U.S. Attorney, W.D. Mich., Mar 11, 2026](https://www.justice.gov/usao-wdmi/pr/2026_0311_Pictured_Rocks_PR); [Upper Michigan's Source, Mar 2026](https://uppermichiganssource.com/2026/03/11/people-caught-vandalising-pictured-rocks-national-lakeshore-fined-banished-one-year)) [VERIFY: I could not read the compendium's collecting section directly]
- **Isle Royale National Park (MI):** No collecting of greenstones, agates, datolite, or other minerals, including those in the Lake Superior waters of the park. ([NPS Isle Royale, Laws and Policies](https://www.nps.gov/isro/learn/management/lawsandpolicies.htm))
- **Apostle Islands National Lakeshore (WI):** Rocks, driftwood, and other natural objects may not be collected. ([NPS Apostle Islands, Laws and Policies](https://www.nps.gov/apis/learn/management/lawsandpolicies.htm))
- **General NPS policy:** ([NPS, Geology Permits](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/geology/permits.htm); [NPS Rock Hunting Guidance, PDF](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/policy/upload/Rock_Hunting_Guidance.pdf))

### Michigan state land, including state parks and Great Lakes shoreline

- **Limit: 25 pounds per person per year, total**, of rocks, mineral specimens, and invertebrate fossils (gold-bearing material excluded), for personal, non-commercial use, from state-owned land. It is a yearly total across all trips, not per visit. ([Michigan DNR, Administrative Rules for Parks and Recreation Areas, PDF](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/-/media/Project/Websites/dnr/Documents/PRD/parks/AdministrativeRules-ParksRecAreas.pdf); [Michigan EGLE, Rock and mineral identification](https://www.michigan.gov/egle/public/learn/geology/rockhounding); [Michigan EGLE, Michigan fossils](https://www.michigan.gov/egle/public/learn/geology/rockhounding/fossils))
- Local parks and townships can set stricter rules. Read the posted signs.
- Penalty: a civil fine of up to $500 is widely reported. [VERIFY: secondary sources only (Michigan Public, WOOD TV)]
- The 25-pound limit is reported to cover Great Lakes bottomlands (stones underwater), and the DNR seized a 93-pound Petoskey stone pulled from Lake Michigan near Northport in 2015. [VERIFY: confirm in DNR or EGLE rule text] ([Michigan Public, 2015](https://michiganpublic.org/offbeat/2015-09-25/petoskey-stone-pulled-out-of-cold-lake-might-land-guy-in-hot-water); [CBS Detroit, state confiscates stone](https://cbsnews.com/detroit/news/state-confiscates-huge-petoskey-stone-man-pulled-from-lake))

### Minnesota

- **No rock collecting in Minnesota state parks, state recreation areas, or Scientific and Natural Areas**, which includes North Shore parks like Gooseberry Falls. (The old Hill Annex Mine State Park exception is gone: the park was abolished in 2024 and closed for mining.) ([MN DNR, Recreational Geology](https://dnr.state.mn.us/geologyrec/index.html); [MN DNR, Geology in state parks](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/education/geology/digging/stateparks.html); [MN DNR, Hill Annex permanently closed, June 2024](https://dnr.state.mn.us/news/2024/06/13/hill-annex-mine-state-park-permanently-closed-allow-mining-resume))

### Wisconsin

- Wisconsin DNR says rock, mineral, and fossil collecting on its properties is limited, banned outright at Havenwoods State Forest and on state trails, and must be **permitted in writing by the property manager**. State Natural Areas require a research permit. ([WI DNR, Gathering in state parks and forests](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/topic/parks/rules/gathering); [WI DNR, State Natural Areas Visitor Guidelines](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/topic/StateNaturalAreas/Visitor_Guidelines); [Wis. Admin. Code NR 45.04](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/code/admin_code/nr/001/45/04/1/c))
- [VERIFY: older and newer NR 45 texts disagree on whether state parks are flatly closed and on daily and yearly weight limits (one version says 5 pounds a day and 50 a year). Read the current NR 45.04 before publishing any number]

### National forests (Hiawatha, Ottawa, Huron-Manistee, Superior, Chequamegon-Nicolet)

- The Forest Service generally allows small-scale, hand collection for personal use, with no selling, no digging machinery, and no collecting in wilderness or closed areas. East of the Mississippi, check with the local ranger district. ([USFS Rockhounding Guide FS-1091, PDF](https://www.fs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/rockhounding-guide-fseprd768373.pdf)) [VERIFY: each forest's local rule]

### Everywhere else

- **Private land:** ask the owner first. Lakefront in front of homes can be private; know where you are walking. [VERIFY: Michigan, Wisconsin, and Minnesota differ on public walking rights along the shore]
- **Tribal land:** ask the tribe. [VERIFY: no source pulled]
- **Never hammer outcrops** in parks or on landmarks like Jasper Knob.
